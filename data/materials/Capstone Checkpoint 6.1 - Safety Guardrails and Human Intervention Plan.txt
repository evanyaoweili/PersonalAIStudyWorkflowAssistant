Capstone Agent Purpose and Main Risks

My capstone project is a Personal AI Study Workflow Assistant. Its purpose is to help a
learner retrieve course information, understand assignment requirements, organize study
activities, and recommend what to work on next. For simple factual questions, the system
can use retrieval to provide a grounded response. For more complex planning questions, it
can use Tree-of-Thought reasoning to compare several possible study plans before
selecting a recommendation.

The main safety and reliability risks include hallucinated information, incorrect
interpretation of assignment requirements, unsupported recommendations, outdated
information, overconfidence, and poor prioritization. The agent could also make an
incorrect recommendation when retrieved evidence is incomplete, ambiguous, or
contradictory. These risks are important because a learner could rely on incorrect
information when making decisions about assignments, deadlines, or study priorities.

Safety Guardrails

I would use several guardrails to constrain and monitor the agent. First, input
validation would determine whether the request is within the study assistant's intended
scope and whether enough information is available to answer safely. Second, RAG
retrieval would ground factual answers in available course materials instead of relying
only on the language model's internal knowledge.

Before an answer is presented, important factual claims would be checked against
retrieved evidence. If the evidence is missing, contradictory, or too weak, the agent
would state its uncertainty, ask for clarification, or escalate instead of inventing an
answer. Tool access would also be restricted. The agent could retrieve, analyze, and
recommend, but it would not automatically submit assignments, modify course records,
send messages, or perform other high-impact actions. Runtime logging would track
retrieval results, confidence levels, decisions, errors, and escalation events.

Evaluation Metrics

I would evaluate the agent using the following metrics:
- Correctness: percentage of answers that accurately reflect the available course information.
- Groundedness: percentage of important claims supported by retrieved evidence.
- Hallucination rate: frequency of unsupported or fabricated claims.
- Calibration: whether the agent's confidence level matches its actual accuracy.
- Escalation accuracy: whether uncertain or risky cases are correctly routed to human review.
- Fallback success rate: how often the system safely asks for clarification or defers when it cannot provide a reliable answer.
- Latency: whether the system provides useful responses within an acceptable amount of time.

Human Intervention Criteria

Human intervention would be required when the agent has low confidence, cannot retrieve
sufficient evidence, finds contradictory sources, or receives an ambiguous request that
could lead to a significantly different recommendation depending on interpretation.
Human review would also be triggered when an important deadline, assignment requirement,
prerequisite, or course policy cannot be verified. If different Tree-of-Thought reasoning
branches produce similarly strong but conflicting recommendations, the system should
present the alternatives and request human judgment rather than selecting one with false
confidence. Any proposed high-impact action, such as submitting work, changing records,
or communicating externally on the learner's behalf, would require explicit human
approval.

Overall Safety Strategy and Design Rationale

The guardrails, evaluation metrics, and human intervention process work together as
layers of protection. Guardrails reduce unsafe behavior before an answer is produced.
Evaluation metrics show whether the system is actually performing reliably over time.
Human intervention provides a final control for uncertain, unusual, or higher-risk
situations.

The main design trade-off is between autonomy and reliability. More autonomy improves
efficiency and reduces the amount of manual work required from the learner, but it also
increases the risk that an incorrect decision will be made without review. Requiring
human approval for every response would improve oversight but would make the system slow
and reduce the benefit of using an autonomous agent. My design therefore uses
confidence-based autonomy. Routine, low-risk, well-grounded requests can be handled
automatically. Low-confidence, ambiguous, conflicting, or high-impact situations are
escalated for human review. This balances autonomy, reliability, efficiency, and
oversight.

Overall, this approach makes the Personal AI Study Workflow Assistant safer and more
dependable for real-world use. The system does not assume that a confident AI response is
necessarily correct. Instead, important recommendations must be grounded in evidence,
evaluated, monitored, and escalated when necessary.
